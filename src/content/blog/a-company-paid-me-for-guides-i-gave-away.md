Last month I got paid by Zo Computer, the company behind the AI computer I use every day. They licensed guides I had already published for free, to use as direct inspiration for their own official tutorials.

I'm not going to tell you the number. It was significant enough that I now treat free documentation as a serious income source instead of a hobby. That's all I'll say about the money.

The guides in question were the beginner ones. I call the collection "Zo Computer 101: the field guide," sixty-six plain-English walkthroughs at zo-101-jeffkazzee.zocomputer.io: how to set up an account, what the agent can actually do, where beginners get stuck. I wrote them because I was using the tool constantly anyway, and nobody had written the guide I wished existed when I started.

I published them publicly, on my own site, under my own name, free to read.

Then someone on the team noticed them. Conversations happened. The deal got done because the work already existed in public, with my name on it.

### Why this worked

First, specificity beats coverage. There are a thousand "getting started with AI" articles written by people who barely use the tools. What companies cannot buy anywhere else is operational detail from somebody who runs their product daily: what breaks, what the docs leave out, what a real workflow looks like after months of use. That's what made my guides worth licensing.

Second, AI agents multiplied the output without multiplying the slop. I used agents to draft, restructure, and check my work, but every claim got verified against the live product before publishing, and every sentence got edited until it sounded like me. The volume came from the machines. The judgment stayed mine. If you publish unverified AI output under your name, you're borrowing credibility you haven't earned. Publish verified work and the same tools make you three times more productive than the writer next to you.

Third, public proof of work is the sales funnel. Publishing for free felt like working without pay. It was actually the portfolio. Companies can't license writing they've never seen, and they'd rather license something that already has a readership than commission something cold.

### How to do this yourself

You don't need permission, credentials, or an audience to start. You need one tool you genuinely use and a place to publish.

Pick the software you already rely on. Write the guide you needed on day one and couldn't find. Include the failures; they're the part that proves you actually did the thing. Publish it under your real name, where search engines and the company itself can find it. Then do it again.

The money doesn't always come from where you expect. Sometimes it's the company itself. Sometimes it's clients who found you through the guide. Sometimes it never comes, and the writing still taught you the tool better than any course would. But if you use any AI tool intensely right now, you are accumulating knowledge that other people, including the people who build that tool, will eventually want.

Total cost of my setup when I started: zero dollars. A free account, a text editor, and opinions backed by actual use. Everything that came after started there.
